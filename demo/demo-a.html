The Slow Return of the Night Train

Sleeper services vanished from most of Europe in the 2000s. A decade of quiet planning has begun to bring them back, one timetable at a time.

The platform at Gare de l'Est fills slowly on a Thursday evening. Passengers arrive with more luggage than a commuter would carry and settle onto benches with the patience of people who have done this before. We had to wait for the bus for nearly an hour before reaching the station, which felt like an unpromising start to a journey advertised as effortless. Nobody around us seemed troubled by it.

The station's own museum of railway history is currently closed and will reopen next Monday, so the display cases of nineteenth-century carriage fittings stay dark behind their glass. A notice taped to the door apologises in four languages. It is a small disappointment, and an oddly fitting one: the night train is itself an exhibit that spent twenty years in storage.

Reviving a sleeper is not romantic work. It is procurement, rolling-stock certification and cross-border path allocation. More than 500 people will attend the conference in Paris next spring where the next tranche of routes gets argued over, and the arguments will be about depreciation schedules rather than about the poetry of waking up in another country.

The legal history is stranger than the engineering. When one national operator withdrew its sleeper service in 2016, a passengers' association challenged the decision on procedural grounds.

The case ground on for three years before a settlement restored two of the six routes, by which point the carriages that had been fought over were already sitting in a siding outside Ostrava with their windows taped up.

One campaigner told me that after failing the exam that would have made him a qualified conductor at nineteen, he felt gloomy all weekend, and then spent the next thirty years defending the trains he never got to drive.

There is a practical case too, and it is unglamorous. A sleeper removes a hotel night and an airport transfer from a trip. Travellers who book through the operator's own site report that the arithmetic works out in their favour on any journey over about eight hundred kilometres. Below that, the aeroplane still wins on time and the coach still wins on price.

  • Vienna to Paris returned in 2021 after a fourteen-year gap.
  • Berlin to Brussels followed, with three services a week.
  • Stockholm to Hamburg runs seasonally, and passengers wait on the platform in weather that would close a British railway.

We are not selling nostalgia. We are selling a bed that moves. Everything else is marketing.

The rolling stock is the bottleneck. New sleeper carriages take four years from order to service, and the order books at the two remaining European manufacturers are full until the end of the decade. Operators are refurbishing 1990s stock instead, stripping interiors back to the shell and rebuilding them with power sockets and better soundproofing. It is cheaper. It is also slower to arrange than anyone expected.

For now the network is thin and the bookings are hard to combine across operators. The people who use these trains do not seem to mind. They arrive early, they find their compartment, and they are asleep before the lights of the suburbs have finished sliding past the window.
