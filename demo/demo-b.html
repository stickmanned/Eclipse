Rebuilding the Riverside Library

Four years after the flood, a small city has finished arguing about what a public reading room is for.

The queue outside the temporary entrance starts before opening. Please wait outside the theater until the doors open, says a sign left over from the building's brief second life as an arts venue, and nobody has taken it down because it still does the job. The doors open at nine. By five past, the reading room is half full.

The collection survived because of a decision nobody wanted to defend at the time. Two months before the water came, staff moved 40,000 volumes to a warehouse on higher ground. Readers who wanted to borrow from the library during that period had to order titles a week ahead and collect them from a portacabin. Complaints were constant. Afterwards, the same arrangement was described in the local paper as foresight.

Across the square, the independent bookstore where most of the city bought its paperbacks did not survive. Its owner had paid for flood insurance that excluded river water, a distinction she says was explained to her once, quickly, in 2009. She now runs a stall inside the library's foyer two mornings a week.

The rebuilt reading room takes its cue from the school next door rather than from any civic monument. The school has 600 pupils and four classrooms given over entirely to reading, and its head teacher sat on the design committee for two years. The result is low shelving, a lot of natural light, and no grand staircase at all.

  • The children's floor opened first, eleven months ahead of the rest.
  • A volunteer was injured in the crash that closed the delivery road for a week.
  • Opening hours were extended after a petition collected 3,000 signatures.

A librarian who has worked here since 1998 is sensitive to criticism of the new layout, and says so plainly. She spent the entire day of the reopening explaining to older readers where the reference section had gone. Most of them, she says, came back the following week and stayed.

People kept asking whether we would still lend things. That was the only question that ever mattered.

The building's running costs are 18 per cent above the old one, which the council absorbed by closing a branch on the eastern edge of the city. A residents' group took the closure to judicial review and lost. Their solicitor has published the ruling in full.

The lawyer filed an appeal after the verdict, and the branch stayed open for the eleven months that the second hearing took. It closed in March. The building is now a dentist's surgery, which several residents mention without being asked.

What the flood settled, in the end, was an argument about function. Nobody in the city now describes the building primarily as an archive. They describe it as somewhere to stay for an afternoon, which is what the queue outside the temporary entrance had been saying all along.
